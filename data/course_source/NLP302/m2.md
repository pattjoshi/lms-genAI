# Word Representations

To capture meaning, words need dense vectors where similar words are close together. This module explains embeddings and the model that made them popular.

## Word Embeddings

Prerequisites: Bag of Words and TF-IDF.

A word embedding represents each word as a dense vector of a few hundred real numbers, for example 300 dimensions, instead of a sparse vector as long as the vocabulary. The values are learned so that words used in similar contexts get similar vectors. This follows the distributional hypothesis: "you shall know a word by the company it keeps".

In embedding space, similarity is measured with cosine similarity, the cosine of the angle between two vectors: 1 means the same direction, 0 means unrelated. "Car" and "automobile" end up close together, something Bag of Words could never capture.

Embedding spaces also capture relationships as directions. The famous example is that the vector for "king" minus "man" plus "woman" lands close to "queen".

Static word embeddings give each word exactly one vector, so "bank" has the same vector in "river bank" and "bank loan". Contextual embeddings from transformer models fix this by producing a different vector for each occurrence depending on its sentence. Sentence and document embeddings extend the idea to whole passages, and they are the basis of semantic search and retrieval-augmented generation.

## Word2Vec

Prerequisites: Word Embeddings, Gradient Descent.

Word2Vec, published by Tomas Mikolov and colleagues at Google in 2013, learns word embeddings with a shallow neural network trained on a simple prediction task over a large text corpus.

It has two variants. CBOW (Continuous Bag of Words) predicts a word from its surrounding context words. Skip-gram does the reverse and predicts the context words from the centre word; it is slower but works better for rare words. The context window, for example 5 words on each side, defines what counts as "nearby".

Predicting over the full vocabulary with softmax is expensive, so Word2Vec uses negative sampling: for each real (word, context) pair, it also samples a few random "negative" words and trains the network to tell real pairs from fake ones. The weights are updated with stochastic gradient descent.

After training, the prediction task is thrown away and the learned weight matrix is kept as the embedding table. GloVe and FastText are popular alternatives; FastText builds vectors from character n-grams, so it can produce embeddings even for unseen words.
